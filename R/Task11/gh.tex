\hypertarget{question-1}{%
\subsection{Question 1}\label{question-1}}

\textbf{\emph{Assumptions}}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  The data is made up of basic random values drawn from the population
\item
  First, we test the hypothesis of normality of the dependent variable
  therefore the price of diamonds
\end{enumerate}

\begin{verbatim}
## 
##  Shapiro-Wilk normality test
## 
## data:  diamonds$price[1:5000]
## W = 0.66175, p-value < 2.2e-16
\end{verbatim}

Since the p-value \textless{} 2.2e-16, implies that data distribution
don't follows the normal distribution.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}
\tightlist
\item
  Next, we will test the hypothesis of equality of variances between the
  groups.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{verbatim}
## 
##  Bartlett test of homogeneity of variances
## 
## data:  diamonds$price by diamonds$cut
## Bartlett's K-squared = 406.7, df = 4, p-value < 2.2e-16
\end{verbatim}

Since the p-value\textless{} 2.2e-16, implies that the variances don't
to be equal.

\textbf{\emph{ANOVA}}

Let m1, m2, m3, m4 and m5 respectively be the mean prices of diamonds
which group are ``Ideal'', ``Premium'', ``Good'', ``Very Good'', and
``Fair''.

H0: m1=m2=m3=m4=m5 Ha: At least one mean different

\begin{verbatim}
## 
##  One-way analysis of means (not assuming equal variances)
## 
## data:  price and cut
## F = 166.04, num df = 4.0, denom df = 9398.6, p-value < 2.2e-16
\end{verbatim}

\emph{Conclusion} With an alpha of 0.05, and a p-value\textless{}
2.2e-16, we reject the null and stat that we have sufficient evidence to
show that there was a effect of cut on diamond prices.

There is no obvious difference in p-value but the F-statistic is
